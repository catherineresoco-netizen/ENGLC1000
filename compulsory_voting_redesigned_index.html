Research Question · Florence, 1494

The Price of Democracy

A Renaissance question with modern consequences: who really governs when only the most motivated citizens show up?

?

Should voting be compulsory to make democracy reflect the broader public rather than disproportionately reflecting highly motivated minorities?

Florence’s struggle between concentrated influence and broader participation frames the core issue behind my essay.

I · V
First Source · Political Philosophy

Voting as Civic Duty

Mill's key claim

“It is strictly a matter of duty.”

Mill argues that voting should not be treated as just another personal choice. Because political decisions affect the whole community, citizens have a responsibility to think beyond themselves.

What this adds

My argument gains an ethical foundation: democracy depends on participation, not just preference.

Takeaway

Rights in a democracy come with obligations to the public good.

II · V
Additional Source · Economic Theory

The Paradox of Not Voting

Timothy J. Feddersen
Individual cost
Collective benefit
From the individual view

One vote is unlikely to change a large election.

From the democratic view

Widespread abstention weakens representation.

What this source changes

Abstaining can be rational even when it creates a collective problem.

Feddersen does not prove that people should stay home. He explains why turnout cannot be taken for granted — and why democracies may need stronger norms or institutions to encourage participation.

This source makes my essay more realistic: people do not always act on civic duty alone.

III · V
Counterargument Source

Freedom vs. Civic Duty

Mill · On Liberty

Individual freedom

Compulsory voting can seem to overstep personal autonomy, especially for citizens who reject every candidate or feel politically uninformed.

“Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.”
⚖
My response

Participation without endorsement

The strongest defense of compulsory voting is limited, not absolute: require participation while preserving privacy, blank ballots, and reasonable exemptions.

Democracy may ask citizens to show up — but not to pretend agreement.
IV · V
What I Learned

My argument became more nuanced.

Where I started

More voters = better democracy.

At first, I assumed that increasing turnout would naturally improve democratic outcomes.

What the research showed
  • Feddersen: abstaining can be rational from the individual perspective.
  • Oprea: broader turnout can shift politics toward the median voter under certain conditions.
  • Fowler: compulsory voting changed turnout and electoral outcomes in Australia.
  • Mill: civic duty still has to be balanced with freedom.
