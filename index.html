If every citizen has a voice, who truly governs when only some choose to use it?

From Renaissance Florence to the modern ballot: should voting be a civic obligation?

I · The moral argument

Voting is a duty.

“It is strictly a matter of duty.”

John Stuart Mill argues that political rights carry obligations to the public—not merely private benefits.

John Stuart Mill · Considerations on Representative Government · Chapter 10

II · The economic paradox

When abstaining
seems rational.

One vote is unlikely to decide an election. Yet democracy depends on millions choosing to participate.

Feddersen explains the individual incentive to abstain; Oprea and colleagues recognize the unequal costs of time and lost wages.

Feddersen (2004) · Oprea, Martin & Brennan (2024)

Ⅱ

III · The strongest objection

Can a free citizen
be compelled?

“Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign.”

A legitimate objection: abstention may express conscience. Blank ballots, secrecy, access and reasonable exemptions protect choice—but do not erase every concern.

John Stuart Mill · On Liberty · Chapter 1

Ⅲ

IV · Evidence changes the argument

Turnout changes
representation.

Anthony Fowler's historical research on compulsory voting in Australia found:

+24 ppvoter turnout
7–10 ppincrease in Labor vote and seat shares

Higher turnout can change election outcomes. It cannot guarantee wiser decisions, moderation, or the same effects in other countries.

Anthony Fowler · Quarterly Journal of Political Science · 2013
